Why is salt put into a dishwasher?
@@ASSISTANT
Salt is put in not to clean the dishes but to keep the water softener in the machine working.

The problem is hard water: calcium and magnesium ions in tap water leave limescale when heated. This scale forms white spots on glasses and deposits on the heating element and hoses, and also reduces the effect of the detergent.

Use coarse-grained dishwasher salt, not table salt. Fine salt and the additives in it can clog the reservoir.
Put the salt only into the salt reservoir, not into the detergent compartment.
Set the machine's hardness setting according to the water hardness in your area. If your water is very soft, salt may not be needed.

Is it legitimate for a criminal judge to fill in vague norms that violate the principle of legality in crimes and punishments?
@@ASSISTANT
In short: No, it is not legitimate for a judge to “fill in” a criminal norm that is vague to an extent that violates the specificity dimension of the principle of legality. However, the distinction between interpretation and creating a norm needs to be drawn well here.

Basis: The principle of legality in crimes and punishments is regulated in Article 38 of the Constitution and Article 2 of the Turkish Criminal Code (TCK); Article 7 of the ECHR gives the same guarantee. The principle has four dimensions: regulation by law (lex scripta), being specific and clear (lex certa), non-retroactivity (lex praevia) and the prohibition of analogy (lex stricta). TCK Art. 2/3 states explicitly that penal provisions cannot be interpreted broadly in a way that leads to analogy.

Interpretation is free, creating norms is forbidden: The judge interprets the existing norm with literal, systematic and purposive methods; it is legitimate for the judge to concretize general but determinable concepts such as “insult” or “obscene.” The ECtHR also accepts that every law needs interpretation; the criterion is that the result be foreseeable for the citizen (for example the Kokkinakis v. Greece judgment). However, if the judge “fills in” the norm by adding an element of a crime or a punishment that is not in the law, or by using analogy, the judge usurps legislative power and violates the principle of legality.

The solution for a truly vague norm: If the norm is so vague that it does not ensure foreseeability, the judge's legitimate course is not to close this gap with a definition of an offense of their own making. The judge either prefers the narrow interpretation in favor of the defendant or considers the provision contrary to the Constitution and brings it before the Constitutional Court through concrete norm review (Constitution Art. 152). The authority to fill the gap belongs to the legislative organ.

In summary: concretizing a general concept within foreseeable limits is legitimate interpretation, whereas a judge's filling in a norm that is vague to an extent violating the principle of legality by adding content is not legitimate.
